CID resilience

Paste a content ID, get a count of real, independent copies. The page asks the public IPFS index who claims to hold the content, downloads a few pieces from each of them, checks the bytes, and times the answers to tell a local copy (served from that provider's own storage) from one fetched on demand (relayed from somewhere else). Free, no account, nothing stored on any server. What it measures and what it can't.

Words used on this page
  • CID: content identifier, the hash-based name of a file or folder on IPFS (the public peer-to-peer file network). Pasting one tells the page what to look for.
  • provider: a server that told the public index (IPNI) it holds the CID. "Advertised" means listed there; it says nothing about whether it really has the bytes.
  • local copy / on demand: a provider that answered a random piece as fast as it answers an empty request is counted as holding the bytes itself; one that was measurably slower probably fetched them from elsewhere first.
  • vantage: the place you measure from, i.e. this browser. Your label and coordinates are attached to your results so that results from different places can be merged.
  • box / network: providers sharing one IP address are one box; providers in one network (an ASN, the number that identifies a hosting company's network) are one operator. Two boxes 1 km apart are not independent, which is why distance matters.
  • baseline / excess: the baseline is how long the provider takes to answer an empty request (a "no-op"); excess is how much longer a real piece took. Large excess means the piece was fetched from somewhere else first.
  • weak timing: the content had fewer than 3 pieces, so each provider got a single test instead of several; treat local/on-demand as a hint, not a verdict. warmed: that provider served the sample first, so its cache was already warm when tested.
  • self-reported: the label and coordinates attached to a result are whatever the person who measured typed in; nothing checks them.
  • proven apart: light in fibre travels about 100 km per millisecond of round trip, so each measured delay bounds how far a provider can be from you. Two providers whose bounds from two different places cannot overlap are proven to sit in different locations.
  • CDN: a content delivery network edge that caches whatever was recently requested; fast, but not durable storage.
What leaves your browser, and what it costs

Free, no account, nothing stored on any server. This browser remembers your last results and location so a reload does not lose them (use "Forget my results" to clear that), and after a run the address bar holds the share link for the results on screen. The CID you paste goes to the public index (cid.contact, delegated-ipfs.dev) and to every provider found; provider hostnames go to Cloudflare DNS and RIPE for IP and network lookup. Your label and coordinates leave only inside a share link you choose to send. If a provider is reachable only over bitswap, the helia library is loaded from jsdelivr (you can turn that off under Settings).

Optional: used only to say how far each copy can be from you, and to merge results from other places. Stays in this browser except inside a share link you send. Button rounds to 0.1 degree (~11 km); typed values are used as given.

"≤ km away" is 102 km per ms of baseline round trip plus 11 km, an upper bound on the responder's distance from that vantage (light in fibre), capped at half the Earth. It is not an estimate of where the responder is. One vantage can never prove two providers are apart; two vantages with non-overlapping disks can. "untested" means no probe ran from that vantage, not that the provider is down.
